**Updated 05/02/26: Closer to “invisible sycophancy” and evaluation story, over mech interp.**

Current methods for evaluating sycophancy in large language models rely entirely on output-based measurement: behavioral tests check whether models abandon correct answers under pressure, LLM-as-judge frameworks score response quality, and multi-turn benchmarks like SYCON-Bench track how quickly models flip positions. These approaches have revealed models abandon correct positions ~50-60% of the time when challenged, with the behavior compounding over multiple prompts. Truth Decay benchmarks show Claude's feedback accuracy degrading from 76.74% to 30.23% by the seventh follow-up. 

As behavioural evals are not causal, lacking awareness of internal model states, they have blindspots where a prompt activate certain behavioural circuits that don’t manifest in outputs. We investigate whether activation-based measurement can complement behavioral evaluation by revealing these "invisible" sycophantic states, and whether such states predict downstream behavioral failures, especially in longer conversational contexts.

We build on recent mechanistic interpretability work proposing sycophancy as a combination of separable components: sycophantic agreement (SyA), genuine agreement (GA), and sycophantic praise (SyPr), encoded as distinct directions in activation space. Replicating the difference-of-means extraction from Vennemeyer et al. (2025) on Llama 3.1 8B Instruct, we achieve classification AUROC scores of 0.91, 0.89, and 0.93 for SyA, GA, and SyPr respectively. Our primary methodological contribution is a dual-point measurement: we compute cosine similarity to sycophancy directions at prompt-end (before generation) and response-end (after generation), with the shift between them quantifying how generation amplifies or suppresses initial behavioral encoding. Applied to a framing experiment, we find that first-person framing ("I believe X") activates sycophancy directions more strongly than third-person framing ("My friend believes X"), even when behavioral outputs are essentially identical; both framings produce correct rejections of false claims. This dissociation between activation state and output suggests that prompts activate sycophantic circuitry which may be suppressed at later layers, for example by safety fine-tuning as indicated by our “astrology” case study. We frame this as relevant to prompt engineers attempting to provide good system prompts, and to inference-time proactive detection of problematic prompts. We hope to extend this to additional concepts like truth, or refusal in future work. 

We propose three experiments to test whether invisible sycophancy is meaningful for evaluation. 

1. We will validate that our extraction methodology scales to larger models by replicating direction extraction on Llama 3.1 70B Instruct using inputs, comparing AUROC scores and framing effect magnitudes across model scales. 
2. We will back-test against Anthropic's SycophancyEval benchmark, which provides ground-truth behavioral labels for approximately 21,000 prompts across four sycophancy types. For each prompt, we will measure prompt-end activation before generating a response, then compare activation magnitude against whether the model's output is behaviorally sycophantic. This directly tests whether high prompt-end activation predicts behavioral sycophancy, and critically, **whether prompts with high activation but non-sycophantic outputs represent fragile suppression that fails under perturbation.** 
3. We will extend to multi-turn evaluation using SYCON-Bench conversation trajectories. We will track activation across conversation turns, testing whether activation magnitude at turn N predicts behavioral flip at turn N+1. If activation-based measurement can anticipate behavioral sycophancy before it manifests, this would establish predictive validity for whitebox evaluation methods.

We will report prompt-end activation distributions stratified by behavioral outcome (sycophantic vs. non-sycophantic), ROC curves for activation-based prediction of behavioral sycophancy, and turn-by-turn activation trajectories for multi-turn conversations that do and do not result in position abandonment. 

Hypothesis:

If activation magnitude reliably predicts behavioral sycophancy, this validates activation monitoring as a complementary evaluation signal, useful for identifying high-risk prompts before deployment. 

If high-activation prompts with non-sycophantic outputs prove fragile under follow-up pressure (flipping at higher rates than low-activation prompts), this would demonstrate that invisible sycophancy has downstream consequences that single- or few-turn output-based evaluation misses.

Conversely, if activation and behavior are uncorrelated, or if invisible sycophancy does not predict fragility, this would suggest that activation-based methods capture processing states rather than evaluatively meaningful risk - a null result that would clarify the appropriate scope of whitebox evaluation, given the layers on which we focus for evaluation. 

It would also raise questions around if we’re looking at the right states (so for example behaviors only expressed at particular layers, or our probes not generalising well / not having been trained on appropriate data [still building this out].

We will compare our activation-based classifications against GPT-4o judge scores on the same prompts to directly quantify agreement and disagreement patterns between whitebox and blackbox methods.
